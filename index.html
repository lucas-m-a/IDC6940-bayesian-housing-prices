Introduction

This study extends the empirical housing price analysis of Morgan, Douthat, and Lin (2022), which examined the relationship between evacuation zone changes and residential property values in Pinellas County, Florida while accounting for different socioeconomic, geographic, and housing characteristics. Morgan, Douthat, and Lin (2022) used OLS with spatial error model (SEM) along with Difference in Differences (DID). The study presented intends to build on that approach by incorporating Bayesian and hierarchical methods and by more directly modeling residual spatial dependence through a spatial autoregressive (SAR) structure. The resulting Bayesian hierarchical spatial model aims to integrate three distinct statistical modeling strategies: hierarchical structure, Bayesian inference, and spatial autoregression.

The hierarchical component is intended to account for the nested and clustered structure of housing data by allowing geographic groups to have their own group level effects and, where applicable, group specified slopes. Galecki and Burzykowski (2013) describe mixed-effects models that allow researchers to “model both the mean structure and the variance-covariance structure of the response variable” while accommodating data where “observations are grouped or clustered”. When considering the study at hand, this approach allows for the relationship between housing prices and selected predictors to vary more appropriately.

The Bayesian framework replaces fixed point estimation with full posterior probability distributions for every parameter, conditional on observed data and specified prior distributions. As Johnson, Ott, and Dogucu (2022) explain, Bayesian methods excel because they “provide a natural framework for updating our understanding of the world as new evidence becomes available, fully capturing parameter uncertainty through posterior distributions rather than relying on asymptotic p-values”. This methodology also allows prior information from existing housing and environmental research to inform model specification. Housing prices may violate the classical assumption of independent observations due to shared location amenities, coastal proximity, and unobserved neighborhood externalities. As Anselin (1988) highlights in his foundational text, “The spatial dimension in economic data implies that observations located in close proximity are dependent, requiring explicit modeling of the spatial error structure to avoid biased standard errors and spurious inferences”. Our Bayesian hierarchical SAR model absorbs latent spatial spillovers allowing for these proximity errors to not affect our estimates.

The literature reviewed for this extension spans hierarchical and mixed-effects models, spatial econometrics, Bayesian methods, and empirical studies of housing markets influenced by storms and environmental occurrences. This motivates the use of a Bayesian hierarchical spatial model for analyzing housing prices.

Housing data naturally possesses hierarchical characteristics due to the grouping of properties within municipalities, evacuation zones, and other geographic units. Therein observations within the groups may contain characteristics that are not entirely captured by the measured predictors. Galecki and Burzykowski (2013) provide a framework for linear mixed-effects models that emphasizes the distinction between fixed and random effects while demonstrating the incorporation of hierarchical structures into regression models. The text includes random intercepts and random slopes that allow for relationships to vary across groups rather than assuming that a singular coefficient applies identically to all observations.

While the hierarchical integration will address potential clustering, spatial statistics will provide the more directed framework for managing geographic dependencies. Anselin (1988) established the foundational methods for spatial econometrics by highlighting spatial dependence and spatial heterogeneity. Spatial dependence occurs when observations in one geographic location are correlated with observations in nearby locations, while spatial heterogeneity is when relationships are differing across geographic spaces.

Spatial econometrics models incorporate the geographic relationships through spatial weight matrices and related spatial processes. Spatial lag and error models provide a mechanism to incorporate dependencies among neighboring observations. As amenity proximity, environmental risks, and market conditions are not restricted to individual properties, evaluating housing prices with spatial concepts becomes exceptionally relevant. This is furthered by LeSage and Pace (2017) in their demonstrations. They show that coefficients in spatial models cannot always be interpreted in the same manner as coefficients from typical regression models. Spatial dependence can contribute direct and indirect effects, and as such a change in a single observation can bleed to neighboring observations through the spatial structure. Their approach with Monte Carlo simulations illustrates how evaluating spatial estimator behaviors becomes more important than relying entirely on conventional interpretations of coefficients. As the objective with the current study is to further account for the geographic structures in that housing market relationships are operating, we will be broadening the scope past identifying significant predictors of the market.

Bayesian methodologies allows for combining the observed data with prior information while more directly representing uncertainty. Johnson, Ott, and Dogucu (2022) is the foundation for Bayesian analysis, ranging from prior distribution specifications to Bayesian models with more complexity.

Being able to combine hierarchical and spatial models makes the Bayesian structure especially effective. Wikle, Zammit-Mangion, and Cressie (2019) illustrate a hierarchical structure for spatial analysis consisting of the observation model, the process model, and the parameter model. This will allow for for the representation of observed data, underlying spatial effects, and uncertain parameters within one framework, thus allowing for the sharing of information while mitigating the uncertainty and error though multiple hierarchical levels.

Sahu (2024) presents similar Bayesian hierarchical methods for spatio-temporal data and further demonstrates how spatial and temporal dependencies can be incorporated into hierarchical models. Spatially correlated observations can be represented through covariance or autoregressive components while the independent measurement error is modeled separately. This distinction in the model structure can be useful in addressing variations in housing prices due to both geographic dependence and property specific variations.

A SAR model provides the final connection between Bayesian hierarchical modeling and spatial econometrics by accounting for the spatial dependence through a spatially lagged output and weights matrix. Within the hierarchical frame, this approach will allow the analysis to distinguish between the property level of predictors and the higher level of geographic predictors.

The empirical literature on housing markets provides valuable insights on geographic and environmental characteristics that are important determinants of housing prices. Bin and Polasky (2004) examined housing prices in North Carolina following Hurricane Floyd using a hedonic property-price framework based on least squares regression. They found that properties located whiting floodplains sold for less than otherwise comparable properties outside of hazardous areas, and subsequently that the difference in discount changed post hurricane. This provides empirical support for the relationship between flood exposure and housing prices, educating our priors while also supporting the use for regression based methods in housing research.

Chandra-Putra and Andrews (2020) similarly examined real estate markets in New Jersey following Hurricane Sandy though using an agent based modeling approach. Their analysis took both the responses to coastal flooding and policies in place for mitigating flooding effects into consideration. Their findings about structural and educational interventions illustrated the interactions between policies, physical risks, and individual behaviors and how the housing market responds. These interactions further suggest that environmental effects may not be adequately represented by singular metrics within a conventional regression structure.

Walls, Magliocca, and McConnell (2018) examined coastal real estate developments using a spatial simulation model that similarly incorporated hurricane risks and coastal amenities. They found that increasing the probability of hurricanes did not reliably produce large changes in the spatial development patterns under the examined conditions while coastal amenities has a stronger influence on location patterns. Although their simulation setting slightly differs from the research at hand, their study demonstrates that environmental risks and spatially distributed amenities should be simultaneously considered when assessing coastal development.

The empirical results of each of these studies is relevant to the present research in that the study area is restricted to south Florida in where hurricane exposure, evacuation zones, flooding, shoreline proximity, and amenities may all contribute to spatial and hierarchical variations in housing prices. The literature therefore informs our priors and suggests that environmental variables should not be evaluated independent from geographical contexts.

Morgan, Douthat, and Lin (2022) provides the primary empirical source for the present study. The study examined approximately 170,000 housing observations and evaluated the relationship between evacuation zone changes and housing prices using ordinary least squares regression with a spatial error component and a difference in differences frame. The analysis also incorporates a broad set of housing, geographic, demographic, and amenity variables. They found several of these characteristics, including distance from shoreline, amenities, and demographic variables to be statistically significantly associated with housing prices.

The present study intends to extend this approach in several ways. The literature on hierarchical modeling suggests that geographic groups may have different baseline housing prices and potentially different relationships between predictors and housing prices. A hierarchical structure therefore may allow coefficients to vary across geographic units rather than imposing a single relationship to the entire study area.

The spatial literature indicates that geographic proximity can create dependence among observations. While the spatial error component used in the original work, a hierarchical Bayesian SAR formulation provides an alternative method by accounting for the spatial dependence within the model. The hierarchical component accounts for variation through geographic groupings while the SAR component represents dependence through neighboring observations. This is particularly relevant as the goal is to estimate housing price relationships while accounting for geographic heterogeneity and spatial dependence.

When evaluated collectively, the resulting Bayesian hierarchical SAR model is therefore motivated by findings complementary to the associated methodological and empirical literature. The hierarchical component will address variation among groups, while the SAR component will capture spatial dependence in housing prices through adjacent observations. The Bayesian approach provides a coherent methodology for estimating these components and quantifying uncertainty.
Rather than assuming that every property will follow an identical independent relationship between the predicting characteristics and price, the proposed model recognizes the geographic structures of housing markets more closely. Properties are embedded within neighborhoods and larger geographic areas, and those locations themselves contain spatial relationships. A coastal housing market in which environmental exposure, evacuation zones, shoreline proximity, amenity integration, and other geographically distributed factors may jointly influence property values.

The literature thus provides a rationale both empirical and methodological for the extension of the existing housing price analysis. Previous research shows an established relationship between environmental risk and property values while the spatial, hierarchical, and Bayesian research provides more flexible and accurate approaches to modeling those relationships. The proposed approach builds on these foundations by incorporating spatial autoregression and hierarchical variation into a Bayesian frame with the objective of obtaining estimates that more appropriately reflect the geographic organization and uncertainty inherent to the housing price data.

Methods

Model Overview

The primary model used in this study is a Bayesian hierarchical simultaneous autoregressive (SAR) spatial error model. Bayesian estimation combines prior distributions for model parameters with observed data to estimate relationships between housing prices and property characteristics. The hierarchical component allows each city to have its own varying intercept, accounting for differences in baseline housing prices across cities. The SAR spatial error component accounts for spatial dependence among nearby properties that may not be explained by the included predictors.

The response variable is the natural logarithm of adjusted sale price, defined as (y_i = (P_i)), where (P_i) represents the adjusted sale price of property (i). The model examines associations between housing prices, proximity to water, property characteristics, and differences across cities while accounting for spatial dependence. The analysis estimates associations rather than causal effects.

Predictor Variables

The explanatory variables include property frontage type, seawall presence, distance to the Coastal Construction Control Line (CCCL), distance to Tampa Bay, property quality, heated living area, acreage, barrier-island status, number of stories, and foundation type. City is included as a hierarchical grouping variable with a varying intercept.

Sale year is treated as a continuous variable centered at 2000:

\[ \text{sale\_year\_c}_i = \text{sale\_year}_i - 2000. \]

This allows the model to estimate a linear association between sale year and log adjusted sale price. A one-unit increase in the centered year variable represents one additional year. The coefficient therefore represents the estimated yearly change in log adjusted sale price, holding the other predictors constant. The analysis includes the sale years represented in the final analytic dataset.

The continuous predictors for CCCL distance, Tampa Bay distance, heated living area, and acreage are standardized by subtracting each variable’s sample mean and dividing by its sample standard deviation. Consequently, their coefficients represent the estimated association between a one-standard-deviation increase in a predictor and log adjusted sale price, holding the other predictors constant.

Categorical variables are represented using indicator (dummy) coding. The reference categories are no frontage, no seawall, average property quality, non-barrier status, one story, and no continuous footing foundation. Coefficients for the other categories estimate differences relative to their respective reference categories, conditional on the remaining predictors in the model.

Hierarchical Model Structure

The hierarchical component of the model is represented by

\[ y_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} + b_{j[i]} + \varepsilon_i. \]

Here, (y_i) is the log adjusted sale price for property (i), (i) is the vector of explanatory variables for that property, () is the vector of regression coefficients, (b{j[i]}) is the city-specific intercept for the city containing property (i), and (_i) is the spatially dependent error for that property.

City-specific intercepts are modeled as

\[ b_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\text{city}}^2). \]

This specification allows cities to have different baseline log-price levels while assuming that their intercepts arise from a shared normal distribution. The parameter (_{}) represents the between-city variation in baseline log-price levels after accounting for the included predictors. This hierarchical structure allows information to be shared across cities rather than fitting an independent regression model for each city.

Spatial Error Structure

Spatial dependence is incorporated through a simultaneous autoregressive (SAR) spatial error process:

\[ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} = \rho W\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + \boldsymbol{\eta}. \]

In this equation, (W) is the spatial weights matrix defining the neighboring relationships among properties, () is the spatial error dependence parameter, and () represents the remaining error component. The parameter () describes the strength of spatial dependence in the error process.

The spatial weights matrix is constructed using an eight-nearest-neighbor procedure based on property coordinates. When the original coordinates are recorded as longitude and latitude, they are projected into an appropriate coordinate reference system for distance calculations before neighboring properties are identified. The neighbor relationships are then symmetrized, and the weights matrix is row-standardized so that the weights associated with each property’s neighbors sum to one.

This SAR spatial error structure accounts for spatial patterns in unexplained housing-price variation among nearby properties. For example, neighboring homes may share unmeasured location-related characteristics. Unlike a spatial lag model, which includes a spatially lagged response variable, the SAR spatial error model represents spatial dependence through the error process rather than directly including neighboring properties’ sale prices as predictors.

Bayesian Estimation

Bayesian estimation combines the likelihood, which describes how probable the observed housing prices are under the model, with prior distributions, which specify assumptions about plausible parameter values before conditioning on the observed data. The model uses a Gaussian likelihood for log adjusted sale price.

The regression coefficients are assigned normal prior distributions, ((0,1)). The intercept is assigned a Student-(t) prior with 3 degrees of freedom, location 11, and scale 2.5. The standard deviations associated with city-level variation and residual variation are assigned Student-(t) priors with 3 degrees of freedom, location 0, and scale 1, subject to the positive constraints on standard deviation parameters. The spatial dependence parameter is estimated as part of the SAR spatial error structure under the model’s specified prior and parameterization.

The model is estimated in R using the brms package and its Stan-based Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling procedure. The sampling configuration uses four chains, 1,000 iterations per chain, and 500 warm-up iterations per chain, with adapt_delta = 0.95 and max_treedepth = 12.

Model convergence and sampling quality are evaluated using the potential scale reduction statistic (()), effective sample sizes, trace plots, and checks for divergent transitions or other sampling warnings. Posterior estimates are summarized using posterior point estimates and 95% credible intervals.

Readings

- Sahu (2024)

This book provides instruction on modeling Spatio-Temporal data using Bayesian methods in R. The section of interest is chapter 7 “Bayesian modeling for point referenced spatio-temporal data”. It outlines three stage hierarchical structure used by Bayesian spatio-temporal models. It discusses how continuous data is modeled using Gaussian models and how spatial and temporal aspects are captured with Gaussian processes and autoregressive models and seperable covariance structures. It goes through the nugget effect, a popular approach to seperating spatio-temporal dependence from independent measurement error. It aslo discusses why seperable models are better for computational efficiency while maintaining prediction accuracy.

- Wikle, Zammit-Mangion, and Cressie (2019)

This book is one of the main references for spatial statistics and has further development on the three hierarchical stages of a model. This is the observation model, the process model, and the parameter model. This lets us combine our observed data with our assumptions while accounting for uncertainty and implement Bayesian reasoning. With this methodology we can share information between time and location points and make better uncertainty estimates with our predictions.

- Galecki and Burzykowski (2013)

This text provides a practical, step-by-step guide to fitting linear mixed-effects (LME) models using R, primarily leveraging the nlme and lme4 packages. It systematically bridges classical linear regression with mixed-effects frameworks, focusing on model formulation, estimation methods (such as Maximum Likelihood and Restricted Maximum Likelihood), and diagnostics for complex data structures.

This book serves as a core reference for hierarchical and multilevel data modeling. It details how to handle nested, repeated-measures, and clustered structures by incorporating both fixed and random effects. Citing this text validates the foundational rules of hierarchical linear models, random intercepts, random slopes, and the explicit modeling of within-group variation.

- Anselin (1988)

Luc Anselin’s foundational text formally introduced spatial econometrics as a distinct field within regional science and quantitative geography. The book establishes the theoretical framework required to model spatial dependence (where observations in one location affect nearby locations) and spatial heterogeneity (where statistical relationships vary across space). It covers spatial autoregressive processes, diagnostic tests for spatial autocorrelation, and estimation techniques like Maximum Likelihood.This text provides the seminal spatial facts and methodological rules for geographically arranged data. Utilizing Anselin’s principles establishes fundamental concepts such as spatial weight matrices (\(W\)), spatial lag models, and spatial error models. These frameworks ensure spatial structure and spatial autocorrelation are properly accounted for rather than treated as simple independence violations.

- LeSage and Pace (2017)

LeSage’s article discusses the misrepresentations coefficients and can produce when assessing spatial models and address shortcomings of their previous research. They instead choose to approach economic data with a Monte Carlo method study and produce a demonstration about the issues of previous approaches and how Monte Carlo circumvents them. For their Monte Carlo instructional they include a branch of Bayesian approach. They conclude that more attention needs to have been brought to the direct and indirect effects estimates and further Monte Carlo practices should consider this for spatial models.

- Walls, Magliocca, and McConnell (2018)

This paper assesses a spatial simulation model of coastal real estate and addressing the influence of storms and amenities (rec access, aesthetics, views, etc). Their model is parameterized to eastern US regions that experience hurricanes at a low rate and then is evaluated to see how spatial patterns of development would change if the frequency of storms was increased. They found that significantly increasing the probability of a hurricane did not change the spatial patterns of development in a meaningful way. If their low hurricane region’s storm possibility was increased to match that of states facing the Gulf of Mexico and all else equal, they found that development continued over time. They found coastal amenities to impact location patterns more. These findings may be important when considering how our own model behaves as Pinellas county is in South Florida and heavily impacted by hurricanes.

- Chandra-Putra and Andrews (2020)

The article assesses real estate prices in two New Jersey towns after hurricane Sandy in 2012. They used an ABM computer simulation model to answer their research questions both about real estate prices in response to coastal flooding and to assess policies in place that may affect or be affected by these floods and aftermaths. They found that preemptive measures such as structural considerations and education had an impact on the amount of claims and fluctuations within the market. This article is helpful for our research because it suggests that spatial statistics may have a bigger impact on our project than originally included.

- Bin and Polasky (2004)

This article has a similar assessment but for a town in North Carolina after hurricane Floyd. They use a “property price function” that is a least squares regression model to assess similar factors rather than a simulation. They overall found a house that is located within a floodplain has a lower cost than a similar and comparable house that is not located in a floodplain. They also found that the discount for existing in the floodplain was higher after hurricane Floyd than before. This article helped see how people are using OLS to model this area of research and compare it to the one used within the article we are expanding on.
