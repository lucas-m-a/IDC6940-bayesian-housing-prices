Bayesian Hierarchical SAR Analysis of Housing Prices in Pinellas County, Florida

Presentation Slides

Author

Lucas Alderfer and Hunter Evans

Introduction

Housing prices reflect a combination of structural characteristics, local amenities, environmental impacts, and broader geographic conditions. In coastal housing markets, it can be substantially more difficult to isolate these factors as properties located close to one another can share these locational conditions and local market pricing information. Properties could therefore show similarities that would not be fully captured by observable characteristics such as size, quality, or shoreline distance. This implication is that housing price models must account for both the directly observable characteristics as well as the geographic organization and residual dependence among nearby properties. This study extends the empirical housing price analysis of Morgan, Douthat, and Lin (2022), which examined the relationship between evacuation zone changes and residential property values in Pinellas County, Florida while accounting for different socioeconomic, geographic, and housing characteristics. Morgan, Douthat, and Lin (2022) used OLS with spatial error model (SEM) along with Difference in Differences (DID).

The study presented intends to build on that approach by retaining the general hedonic housing price framework while incorporating Bayesian and hierarchical methods and by extending the treatment of geographic variation and residual spatial dependence. Rather than modeling the spatial component through conventional spatial error, the proposed model represents residual spatial dependence through a continuous latent spatial field. This field is modeled using a Matérn covariance structure and stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) approach. Bayesian inference is performed using the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) framework. The resulting Bayesian hierarchical spatial model aims to integrate three distinct statistical modeling strategies: hierarchical variation among municipalities, Bayesian inference, and a continuous spatial random field. The hierarchical component allows properties located within the same municipality to share a municipality specific random intercept as such recognizing that municipalities may differ in their baseline housing prices after observed housing characteristics being accounted for. The spatial component represents the remaining geographic dependencies across the study’s regions through a latent spatial process evaluated at each property’s coordinates. Municipality effects capture systematic differences associated with geographic group membership whereas the continuous spatial field captures residual spatial dependence that may remain after those municipality level differences and observed predictors are accounted for.

Properties within the same municipality may share characteristics that are difficult to measure directly. Mixed effects models allow these grouped observations ot be modeled while distinguishing population level effects from a group level variation. Galecki and Burzykowski (2013) describe mixed-effects models as capable of representing boht the mean structure and variance covariance structure of responses when observations are grouped or clustered. When considering the study at hand, this approach motivates the inclusion of municipality specific random intercepts rather than the assumption that each municipality would have the same baseline housing prices.

The hierarchical structure also allows for more appropriate variation at the municipality level, as this variation is not interchangeable with the continuous spatial dependence. A municipality random effect represents the difference between geographic groups where a spatial random field represents geographic dependence across the coordinate space itself. Two observed properties may be geographically close but still located in different municipalities and may therefore share unobserved spatial characteristics. Conversely, properties could be in the same municipality yet have a more substantial geographic distance separating them. Modeling both of these structures concurrently provides a more flexible representation of the geographic organization of the coastal housing market. Spatial dependence is also a concern in that the assumption that observations are independent could be unrealistic. Properties in close proximity can we affected by common neighborhood amenities, infrastructures, environmental exposure, and other influencing conditions. Anselin (1988) established the importance of explicit accountancy of the spatial dependencies and spatial heterogeneity in economic data. Spatial dependence refers to relationships among observations that are associated with their geographic proximity to each other, while spatial heterogeneity concerns the differences in relationships across geographic locations. These concepts reflect that property value is in part determined by characteristics of the surrounding environment and locale.

The spatial component of the model proposed is based on a Matérn Gaussian random field. Matérn models provide a more accommodating representation of spatial covariance through parameters that govern the magnitude and spatial range of dependence. The SPDE formulation developed for spatial modeling provides a more computationally efficient approach by expressing the continuous spatial process through a sparse Gaussian Markov random field defined over a triangulated spatial mesh (Lindgren, Rue, and Lindström (2011)). This approach became necessary as the dataset contains more than 260,000 property observations. Direct covariance based calculations attempted became too computationally difficult for the full dataset to move forward with more traditional Markov Chain approaches. The SPDE approach therein allows for the modeling of the geographic dependence and accommodating the computational requirements of the housing prices data. The mesh does not represent a a sample of observations but rather a finite dimensional representation of the latent spatial field. The full dataset of property observations can therefore contribute to the estimations while the latent spatial process is represented by a smaller collection of mesh vertices. The projection matrix used by INLA maps the latent spatial field from the mesh to the coordinates of the unobserved properties.

Bayesian hierarchical modeling provides a comfortable framework for integrating these components while representing uncertainty in both fixed and latent effects. Instead of producing only point estimates for model parameters, Bayesian inference produces posterior distributions conditional on the observed data and specified priors. This lets uncertainty be quantifiable for fixed effects, municipality level random effects, the spatial field, and the hyperparameters overseeing the spatial process. Prior distributions can also be used to regularize more complex model components when estimating the latent spatial processes. Bayesian hierarchical spatial models are expressed through multiple levels. At our observation level, observed housing prices are modeled conditional on a linear prediction and an error distribution. In the process level, latent municipality effects and the continuos spatial field described unobserved geographic variation. At the parameter level, prior distributions inform the regression coefficients and hyperparameters associated with latent processes. Wikle, Zammit-Mangion, and Cressie (2019) describe hierarchical spatial models in this three part system of the observation, process, and parameter levels to separate measurement variation from latent spatial processes and parameter uncertainty.

For the present study, the computational implementation of the proposed model used Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation. INLA was developed as a computationally efficient approach for Bayesian inference in latent Gaussian models and provides an alternative to computational intensive Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation methods for a wide range of hierarchical spatial models (Fuglstad et al. (2019)). Martins et al. (2013) provides a basis of demonstration for the use of R-INLA with spatial and spatio-temporal models and describe its application to models containing spatially structured latent effects. The proposed model combines a Gaussian observation model, hierarchical random effects, and a latent spatial process within the INLA methodology. Combining INLA and SPDE is therefore both computationally convenient and allows for a continuous spatial Bayesian model to be feasible for the large dataset. The SPDE formulation converts the latent Matérn field into a spare representation while INLA provides approximate posterior inferences for the resulting model. The resulting combination provides both spatial prediction and uncertainty quantifications while bypassing restrictions placed by the computational demand of dense covariance matrices.

Empirical housing literature reviewed provides justification for the inclusion of environmental and geographic characteristics in the model. Bin and Polasky (2004) examined property values in North Caroline following Hurricane Floyd and found evidence to suggest that properties located within floodplains experienced more substantial pricing decreases relative to otherwise comparable properties outside of flood hazard areas. These findings help demonstrate the reflection of environmental exposures on housing markets and provide support for including similar measures within the present study. Coastal amenities provide another consideration to the spatial structure surrounding housing prices. Walls (2018) examined coastal development while incorporating hurricane risks and coastal amenities into a spatial simulation. The results indicated that coastal amenities have the potential to substantially influence development patterns even if hurricane risks are increased. Environmental characteristics may simultaneously represent exposures and location amenities. Coastline proximity, water features, and other preparatory features can therefore have effects that depend on the geographic context of the characteristics.

These findings have a strong relevance within our area of focus in Pinellas County, Florida. Housing values in Pinellas may reflect a combination of coastal amenities, environmental conditions, and other characteristics observed within the data. The same geographic features that create environmental risks can also provide or incentivize amenities that subsequently increase residential demands. Morgan, Douthat, and Lin (2022) provides the primary empirical source for the present study. The study examined approximately 170,000 housing observations and evaluated the relationship between evacuation zone changes and housing prices using ordinary least squares regression with a spatial error component and a difference in differences frame. The analysis also incorporates a broad set of housing, geographic, demographic, and amenity variables. They found several of these characteristics, including distance from shoreline, amenities, and demographic variables to be statistically significantly associated with housing prices.

The present study therefore provides a methodological contribution to the original research. The literature thus provides a rationale both empirical and methodological for the extension of the existing housing price analysis. Previous research shows an established relationship between environmental risk and property values while the spatial, hierarchical, and Bayesian research provides more flexible and accurate approaches to modeling those relationships. The proposed approach builds on these foundations by incorporating the latent spatial field and hierarchical variation into a Bayesian frame with the objective of obtaining estimates that more appropriately reflect the geographic organization and uncertainty inherent to the housing price data.

Methods

This study investigates the determinants of residential housing prices in Pinellas County, Florida, utilizing a sample of around 245,000 house sales spanning the years 2000 through 2015 by using the Bayesian framework. To account for both large scale spatial structures and local spatial differences in pricing, the analysis employs Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (INLA) coupled a hierarchical structure varying by city. The primary spatial model is specified as a full Bayesian spatial Matérn SPDE model incorporating city level random effects.

Variable Specifications and Transformations

The response variable is the natural logarithm of the adjusted sale price (adjusted by the Case-Shiller Index) , denoted as \(y_i = \ln(P_i)\) for property \(i\). Modeling log-adjusted prices stabilizes variance and allows for the model to accommodate properties ranging from low-cost homes in the multi-millions. The set of explanatory variables captures structural property characteristics, environmental and hazard proximity, and temporal trends:

  • Temporal Trend: Sale year is modeled as a continuous linear trend centered at the year 2000 (sale_year_c = sale_year - 2000), where one unit represents a one-year increment.

  • Proximity Measures: Distances to the Coastal Construction Control Line (cccl_distance) and Tampa Bay (tb_distance) are included. These continuous variables—along with heated living area (heated_area) and property acreage (acreage)—are standardized to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one.

  • Property and Site Characteristics: Categorical indicators include frontage type (frontage_group: None [reference], Lake, Bay, Other), seawall presence (seawall_group: No [reference], Yes), property quality (quality_group: Average [reference], Fair, Above Average), number of stories (stories_group: 1 [reference], 2, 3+), continuous foundation footing (foundation_group: No [reference], Yes), barrier-island municipality status (barrier_municipality: Non-Barrier [reference], Barrier), and municipality identifier (site_city).

Bayesian Spatial SPDE Model Specification

The spatial model estimates housing price associations while simultaneously capturing city baseline differences and continuous spatial dependence via a GMRF:

\[y_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top \boldsymbol{\beta} + b_{j[i]} + \xi(\mathbf{s}_i) + \epsilon_i\] \[\mathbf{b} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}, \tau_{\text{city}}^{-1} \mathbf{I}\right), \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}, \tau_\epsilon^{-1} \mathbf{I}\right)\]

where:

\(\mathbf{x}_i\): Vector of standardized continuous predictors and categorical dummy indicators for property \(i\).

\(\boldsymbol{\beta}\): Vector of fixed-effect regression coefficients.

\(b_{j[i]} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{city}})\): Varying intercept for city \(j\) containing property \(i\), capturing baseline city price differences.

\(\xi(\mathbf{s}_i)\): Continuous spatial random effect evaluated at property geographic coordinates \(\mathbf{s}_i = (x_i, y_i)\) using a Matérn SPDE representation.

\(\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_\epsilon)\): Independent Gaussian residual error term.

SPDE Mesh Construction and Spatial Gaussian Process

Property coordinates \((x_i, y_i)\) are projected to Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM Zone 17N, meters) to ensure accurate Euclidean distance. Due to the computation needed to calculate a dense covariance matrix across all \(N = 245,477\) point locations, the model uses continuous spatial process \(\xi(\mathbf{s})\). It is represented as a Gaussian Random Field with a Matérn covariance function and discretized using the Lindgren et al. (2011) SPDE approach:

\[(\kappa^2 - \Delta)^{\alpha/2} \left( \tau \xi(\mathbf{s}) \right) = \mathcal{W}(\mathbf{s})\]

where \(\Delta\) is the Laplacian operator, \(\alpha = \nu + d/2\) governs smooth parameterization (set to \(\alpha = 2\) for \(d = 2\) spatial dimensions), \(\kappa > 0\) is the spatial scale parameter, \(\tau\) controls spatial variance, and \(\mathcal{W}(\mathbf{s})\) is spatial Gaussian white noise (Lindgren et al. (2011).

A constrained, two-dimensional refined Delaunay triangulation (SPDE mesh) is constructed over Pinellas County using the INLA package’s function INLA::inla.mesh.2d. The mesh defines \(K\) connecting vertices across the domain, then extends to the surrounding areas to reach the edges of the map so there is no boundary bias. The continuous spatial field is represented as piecewise linear basis functions \(\psi_k(\mathbf{s})\) defined over the vertices. This formulation also comes from Lindgren et al. (2011).

\[\xi(\mathbf{s}) = \sum_{k=1}^K A_{ik} u_k\]

where \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1, \dots, u_K)^\top \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{Q}^{-1})\) is a Gaussian Markov Random Field vector on the mesh nodes with sparse precision matrix \(\mathbf{Q}(\kappa, \tau)\), and \(\mathbf{A}\) is the sparse spatial projection matrix mapping mesh vertices \(k\) to observation coordinates \(i\) (Lindgren et al. (2011)).

Bayesian Estimation and Diagnostics

Unlike traditional Bayesian methods that use computationally intensive MCMC sampling, the model is estimated via deterministic numerical integration using R-INLA (INLA package version 25.10.19) with the simplified Laplace approximation strategy. Model performance and predictive accuracy are evaluated using goodness-of-fit metrics including Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE).

Readings

- Sahu (2024)

This book provides instruction on modeling Spatio-Temporal data using Bayesian methods in R. The section of interest is chapter 7 “Bayesian modeling for point referenced spatio-temporal data”. It outlines three stage hierarchical structure used by Bayesian spatio-temporal models. It discusses how continuous data is modeled using Gaussian models and how spatial and temporal aspects are captured with Gaussian processes and autoregressive models and seperable covariance structures. It goes through the nugget effect, a popular approach to seperating spatio-temporal dependence from independent measurement error. It aslo discusses why seperable models are better for computational efficiency while maintaining prediction accuracy.

- Wikle, Zammit-Mangion, and Cressie (2019)

This book is one of the main references for spatial statistics and has further development on the three hierarchical stages of a model. This is the observation model, the process model, and the parameter model. This lets us combine our observed data with our assumptions while accounting for uncertainty and implement Bayesian reasoning. With this methodology we can share information between time and location points and make better uncertainty estimates with our predictions.

- Galecki and Burzykowski (2013)

This text provides a practical, step-by-step guide to fitting linear mixed-effects (LME) models using R, primarily leveraging the nlme and lme4 packages. It systematically bridges classical linear regression with mixed-effects frameworks, focusing on model formulation, estimation methods (such as Maximum Likelihood and Restricted Maximum Likelihood), and diagnostics for complex data structures.

This book serves as a core reference for hierarchical and multilevel data modeling. It details how to handle nested, repeated-measures, and clustered structures by incorporating both fixed and random effects. Citing this text validates the foundational rules of hierarchical linear models, random intercepts, random slopes, and the explicit modeling of within-group variation.

- Anselin (1988)

Luc Anselin’s foundational text formally introduced spatial econometrics as a distinct field within regional science and quantitative geography. The book establishes the theoretical framework required to model spatial dependence (where observations in one location affect nearby locations) and spatial heterogeneity (where statistical relationships vary across space). It covers spatial autoregressive processes, diagnostic tests for spatial autocorrelation, and estimation techniques like Maximum Likelihood.This text provides the seminal spatial facts and methodological rules for geographically arranged data. Utilizing Anselin’s principles establishes fundamental concepts such as spatial weight matrices (\(W\)), spatial lag models, and spatial error models. These frameworks ensure spatial structure and spatial autocorrelation are properly accounted for rather than treated as simple independence violations.

- LeSage and Pace (2017)

LeSage’s article discusses the misrepresentations coefficients and can produce when assessing spatial models and address shortcomings of their previous research. They instead choose to approach economic data with a Monte Carlo method study and produce a demonstration about the issues of previous approaches and how Monte Carlo circumvents them. For their Monte Carlo instructional they include a branch of Bayesian approach. They conclude that more attention needs to have been brought to the direct and indirect effects estimates and further Monte Carlo practices should consider this for spatial models.

- Walls, Magliocca, and McConnell (2018)

This paper assesses a spatial simulation model of coastal real estate and addressing the influence of storms and amenities (rec access, aesthetics, views, etc). Their model is parameterized to eastern US regions that experience hurricanes at a low rate and then is evaluated to see how spatial patterns of development would change if the frequency of storms was increased. They found that significantly increasing the probability of a hurricane did not change the spatial patterns of development in a meaningful way. If their low hurricane region’s storm possibility was increased to match that of states facing the Gulf of Mexico and all else equal, they found that development continued over time. They found coastal amenities to impact location patterns more. These findings may be important when considering how our own model behaves as Pinellas county is in South Florida and heavily impacted by hurricanes.

- Chandra-Putra and Andrews (2020)

The article assesses real estate prices in two New Jersey towns after hurricane Sandy in 2012. They used an ABM computer simulation model to answer their research questions both about real estate prices in response to coastal flooding and to assess policies in place that may affect or be affected by these floods and aftermaths. They found that preemptive measures such as structural considerations and education had an impact on the amount of claims and fluctuations within the market. This article is helpful for our research because it suggests that spatial statistics may have a bigger impact on our project than originally included.

- Bin and Polasky (2004)

This article has a similar assessment but for a town in North Carolina after hurricane Floyd. They use a “property price function” that is a least squares regression model to assess similar factors rather than a simulation. They overall found a house that is located within a floodplain has a lower cost than a similar and comparable house that is not located in a floodplain. They also found that the discount for existing in the floodplain was higher after hurricane Floyd than before. This article helped see how people are using OLS to model this area of research and compare it to the one used within the article we are expanding on.
